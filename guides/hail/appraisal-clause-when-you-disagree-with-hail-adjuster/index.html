The appraisal clause: what to do when you disagree with the hail adjuster's number

Published 2026-11-24

The adjuster came out, agreed the hail damage is covered, and wrote an estimate. Your roofer's estimate, or your body shop's or PDR tech's estimate for the car, is a lot higher. The adjuster won't budge. Now what?

Many Texas home and auto policies have a built-in way to settle exactly this kind of fight without a lawsuit: the appraisal clause.

The short version: appraisal is for disputes about how much the covered damage costs, not whether it's covered. You and the insurer each hire your own appraiser, the two appraisers pick a neutral umpire, and an amount agreed by the appraisers (or by one appraiser and the umpire) is binding on both sides. You pay your own appraiser and half of the umpire's cost. Texas made an appraisal provision mandatory in personal auto and home policies in 2025, and new TDI rules spell out deadlines for policies issued or renewed on or after March 1, 2027.

First, ask the insurer to look again

Before appraisal, TDI's advice for both home and auto claims is the same: tell the company why you disagree and send proof. From TDI's “What if my insurance isn't paying enough?” page: “The company may have overlooked something and may make adjustments. Make sure to send any supporting documentation, such as a contractor's estimate for the repairs.” For a car, TDI's auto complaint page says to send “the body shop's estimate for the repairs.”

Line-by-line comparisons help. If the insurer's estimate leaves out a vent, a ridge cap, a panel, or a dent count your repairer found, point to the exact line.

What appraisal decides, and what it doesn't

TDI's home insurance guide: “The appraisal process is for disputes about the amount of your claim. It isn't for disputes about whether your policy covers a loss.”

The new Texas law says the same thing. Insurance Code Sec. 1813.003 describes the required appraisal provision as a dispute resolution process “solely to determine the amount of loss when that amount is in dispute between the policyholder and the insurer.”

So appraisal fits when:

  • The insurer agrees hail damaged your roof or car, but you disagree on the repair cost, the number of damaged panels, or what it takes to fix them.

It doesn't settle:

  • A flat denial, such as “this is wear and tear, not hail,” or “this damage happened before your policy started.” That's a coverage dispute. TDI's auto page notes that if there's a coverage question, “you can try to resolve that issue after the appraisal.” For a denied roof claim, see Why roof hail claims get denied.

How appraisal works, step by step

TDI describes the basic process on its disagree page:

1. You or the insurer demands appraisal. Do it in writing and keep a copy. 2. Each side hires its own appraiser. 3. The two appraisers choose a third appraiser as the umpire. 4. Each appraiser estimates the loss. If they agree, that's the amount. 5. If they don't agree, the umpire decides, and “the umpire makes the final decision, which is binding on both you and the company.”

What it costs

TDI: “You are responsible for the expenses of the appraiser you hire and for half of the umpire's expenses.” TDI doesn't publish typical fees, and neither do we. Ask any appraiser you're considering how they charge, in writing, before you sign.

TDI suggests that if you need help finding an appraiser, you can search for a “property damage appraiser” or check with your contractor.

What real policy language looks like

Here is part of the appraisal condition in one real Texas home policy, the Texas FAIR Plan Association homeowners policy (edition date June 1, 2018):

“If you and we fail to agree on the amount of loss, either may demand an appraisal of the loss. In this event, each party will choose a qualified appraiser and notify the other of the appraiser's identity within 20 days after receiving a written request from the other.”

“Each party will pay its own appraiser and bear the other expense of the appraisal and umpire equally. Any fees for expert witnesses or attorneys will be paid by the party who hires them.”

Your policy's wording may be different. Look in the Conditions section of your home policy, or the physical damage part of your auto policy, for a heading called Appraisal, and read it start to finish, especially any deadline.

What changed in Texas law

SB 458 (2025): appraisal is now required in home and auto policies

Senate Bill 458 added Insurance Code Chapter 1813, “Appraisal of Disputed Losses,” effective September 1, 2025.

  • It applies to personal automobile and residential property policies, including those from stock and mutual companies, county mutuals, Lloyd's plans, reciprocal exchanges, farm mutuals, certain surplus lines insurers, and the Texas FAIR Plan.
  • It does not apply to Texas Windstorm Insurance Association (TWIA) policies or commercial policies.
  • Those policies “must contain an appraisal provision that complies with this chapter” (Sec. 1813.003).
  • Under Sec. 1813.004, the amount set by appraisal is binding on you and the insurer “except for fraud, accident, or material mistake relevant to the appraisal or an appraisal award made without authority.”

TDI's Bulletin B-0012-25 summarized SB 458 as the “right to home and auto appraisal” and said it “applies to forms issued or renewed after January 1, 2026.”

TDI's new appraisal rules: deadlines starting with policies issued or renewed March 1, 2027

On November 6, 2026, TDI adopted new rules, 28 TAC §§5.9800-5.9806, in Commissioner's Order No. 2026-10210. They apply to personal auto and residential property policies “delivered, issued for delivery, or renewed on or after March 1, 2027.” Until your policy is issued or renewed under them, your current policy's appraisal clause controls, so read it.

Under the adopted rules, the appraisal provision in a covered policy must:

  • State that either party can demand appraisal on its own, and not require the two sides to reach an “impasse” first.
  • Cover disputes about the amount of loss, including partial or total loss.
  • Make the award binding as to the amount of loss unless it was made without authority, wasn't made in substantial compliance with the appraisal provision, or involved fraud, accident, or material mistake.

The insurer also has to send you a plain-language appraisal process notice at the same time it tells you it is accepting or rejecting your claim. It must explain where the appraisal clause is in your policy, how to demand appraisal, your responsibilities (including hiring an appraiser and expenses), how to get an umpire appointed, the deadlines, and the effect of the award.

The rules set these deadlines after a written appraisal demand:

  • Hire appraisers: within 20 days, each side hires its own appraiser and sends the other side the appraiser's name and contact information in writing (home and auto).
  • Pick an umpire: the two appraisers must jointly choose an umpire within 15 days after that step, or after they fail to agree on the amount (home and auto).
  • Try to agree on the amount: the appraisers have until 120 days after the demand for a home claim, or 40 days for an auto claim.
  • Award when an umpire is involved: due no later than 240 days after the demand for a home claim, or 180 days for an auto claim. If it isn't issued by then, the umpire is replaced.

Other points from the adopted rules:

  • When to demand. Generally no later than the end of the policy's deadline for filing a lawsuit. Different rules apply once a lawsuit is filed.
  • If the appraisers can't agree on an umpire, either side can ask a county or district court to appoint one: where the home is located, or where the car is principally garaged. A policy may also offer an independent umpire-selection vendor, but only the policyholder can choose that option. The side asking for an appointment must give the other side written notice at least 10 days before the request.
  • Who can serve. Appraisers and umpires must be competent by training or experience to evaluate the type of loss, independent from the parties, and disinterested in the outcome. For damage to a dwelling, they must also be an adjuster or public adjuster with residential estimating experience or training, an engineer or architect with relevant residential experience, or someone with occupational experience or training in building, repairing, or estimating that type of property loss.
  • Changing deadlines. The two sides can change any deadline by written agreement after the insurer has sent the appraisal process notice.

TWIA policies are different

TWIA wind and hail policies (mostly on the coast) are excluded from Chapter 1813 and have their own appraisal rules. TDI's disagree page explains TWIA's process and deadlines, including a 60-day window to demand appraisal after TWIA's estimate on older policies or policies without replacement cost coverage.

Appraisal vs. your other options

TDI lists several paths when you disagree. They aren't mutually exclusive.

  • Complain to TDI. If you think the company treated you unfairly, you can file a complaint with TDI. TDI's Help Line is 800-252-3439.
  • Hire a public adjuster (home claims). TDI: “Some people hire a public insurance adjuster if they disagree with the number or type of damages listed by the insurance company's adjuster. Public adjusters charge fees for their services.”
  • Mediation, which TDI describes as using “a neutral third party to settle disputes outside court.”
  • Go to court. For weather-related property claims like hail, Texas generally requires written notice at least 61 days before you sue, under Insurance Code Sec. 542A.003. TDI's home guide puts it simply: “If the damage was caused by a disaster, you must tell the company in writing at least 61 days before you sue.” For legal questions, talk to a Texas attorney.

A quick checklist before you demand appraisal

1. Confirm it's an amount dispute, not a denial of coverage. 2. Find the Appraisal section of your policy and note any deadline, notice address, and cost split. 3. Gather your evidence: the insurer's estimate, your contractor's or repair shop's itemized estimate, photos, and the adjuster's notes or letters. 4. Send the demand in writing to the address your policy or claim letters give, include your claim number, and keep a copy and proof of delivery. 5. Ask any appraiser how they charge, what experience they have with hail claims like yours, and whether they have any connection to you, your contractor, or the insurer. 6. Track the deadlines in your policy (and, for policies issued or renewed on or after March 1, 2027, the TDI deadlines above).
