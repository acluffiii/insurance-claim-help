Hail claims

Why roof hail claims get denied, and what official Texas help looks like

Published 2026-11-19

A roof claim after a hail storm is one of the most common home claims in Texas, and a denial letter is one of the most common things people post about afterward. The letter often feels final. It usually isn't the end of the road, but the options depend on why the claim was denied.

The short version: a roof hail claim is usually turned down for one of a few reasons — the insurer decided the damage is wear and tear rather than hail, the policy doesn't cover the roof the way you thought, the loss is under the deductible, or a policy condition (like a filing deadline) wasn't met. In Texas the company has to tell you why in writing. What you do next depends on whether you disagree about the facts, the amount, or the coverage.

Common reasons a roof claim gets turned down

These come from what TDI's consumer pages say policies do and don't pay for. Your policy's wording is what actually controls, so read the denial letter next to your policy.

  • "Wear and tear," not hail. TDI's home insurance guide lists wear and tear among the things most home policies don't cover. TDI's roof tip puts it plainly: "your insurance company won’t pay for a new roof just because it’s old or worn out." Many roof denials come down to this question — whether the marks on the shingles were caused by the storm or by age.
  • The roof coverage changed or is limited. The same roof tip says that "As roofs age, some companies will switch to actual cash value," and that "if your roof is in poor condition, your company might not cover your roof at all." It says your company should tell you when it changes your coverage, so check your renewal paperwork and declarations page.
  • The damage is less than the deductible. TDI's roof tip suggests asking whether your wind and hail deductible is different from the deductible for other kinds of damage. A higher wind/hail deductible can mean an estimate that comes in under the deductible and pays nothing. Our guide on hail coverage and deductibles explains how to find yours.
  • Hail isn't covered on this policy. TDI's home guide says most policies cover windstorm, hurricane, and hail, "but not if you live on the Gulf Coast." Coastal homes may need wind and hail coverage through the Texas Windstorm Insurance Association (TWIA) instead.
  • A filing deadline or other condition. TDI's home guide says "Most companies have deadlines for you to file a claim. Some policies have a one-year deadline unless you can show good cause for the delay." It also warns, "The insurance company might deny your claim if you make permanent repairs before it sees the damage." Temporary repairs, such as a tarp, are what TDI recommends.

What the company has to tell you

TDI's claim-steps tip and home guide describe the deadlines Texas law sets once you've filed:

  • The company has 15 days (the claim-steps tip says 15 business days) to acknowledge your claim, start its review, and ask for what it needs.
  • It then has 15 business days after getting what it needs to accept or reject the claim. It can extend that by 45 days if it tells you why, and TDI can extend these deadlines by 15 more days after a weather-related catastrophe.
  • "If the company rejects your claim, it must say why in writing."

That written reason is the most useful document you have. Ask for it if you don't have it, along with a copy of the adjuster's estimate or inspection findings.

Step 1: Go back to the company with evidence

TDI's adjuster tip explains that the adjuster "works for the insurance company paying the claim." If you disagree, TDI says to "tell the insurance company why. They may adjust the offer." TDI's homeowners complaint page adds: "Send the company any supporting documents such as a contractor’s estimate for the repairs."

Practical points from TDI's hail and home-claim pages:

  • Photos and video of the damage, taken before any repairs, and a list of damage inside and outside.
  • Being there for the inspection. TDI's home guide says "It’s a good idea to have your contractor with you."
  • Notes on every call: who you talked to and when.
  • If you can't work things out with the adjuster, TDI says "You can ask the company to assign a different adjuster."

Step 2: Know what appraisal can and can't do

Many home policies have an appraisal clause. TDI's “isn't paying enough” tip explains that you and the company each hire an appraiser, the two choose an umpire, and the umpire's decision "is binding on both you and the company." You pay for your own appraiser and half of the umpire's expenses, and you should check your policy for any deadline to demand appraisal.

The catch for a denied claim: TDI's home guide says "The appraisal process is for disputes about the amount of your claim. It isn’t for disputes about whether your policy covers a loss." If the company says the roof damage isn't covered at all, appraisal may not be the right tool. If the company agreed some hail damage is covered but paid for a few shingles instead of a roof, that's a dispute about amount.

TDI also notes that some people hire a public insurance adjuster, who works for you, not the insurer, and charges a fee. TDI says public adjusters must have a TDI license, and you can check one through TDI's Help Line or its agent lookup.

Step 3: File a complaint with TDI — and know its limits

TDI's complaint page says it can help with complaints against insurance companies, agents, and adjusters it regulates. The homeowners page explains the process: you file online with your documents, TDI contacts the company, and "Companies have 15 days to respond to us and can ask for a 10-day extension."

TDI is clear about what it can't do, and it uses the roof question as its example. It can't "Make a company pay a claim unless the failure to pay violates a law or the terms of your policy," and it can't "Settle disputes when you and the company disagree about the facts. For example, we can’t determine whether damage was caused by a storm or normal wear and tear." It also can't handle complaints about roofers or contractors.

So a TDI complaint is most useful when the company missed a deadline, didn't explain the denial, or didn't follow the policy. A pure "hail vs. wear and tear" disagreement usually has to be settled with evidence, appraisal if it's about amount, or in court.

Step 4: Legal help, and the 61-day notice

If none of that resolves it, TDI says you can sue. Its home guide adds a Texas-specific step: "If the damage was caused by a disaster, you must tell the company in writing at least 61 days before you sue," unless waiting would make you miss the deadline to file. TDI's home guide also mentions Justice Court for claims under $20,000, and the "isn't paying enough" tip links to resources for finding legal help and mentions mediation as an option. This site can't tell you whether you have a case; a Texas attorney can.

Before you cash a check or sign anything

If a partial denial came with a check, TDI's disaster-claims FAQ says to "Talk to your company before you sign the check. Some companies consider your claim final and settled if you sign the check."

Avoid making it worse

TDI's hail tip warns against contractors who offer to waive your deductible: "It’s illegal to waive a homeowners deductible under state law." Get more than one estimate, don't pay in full up front, and never sign a contract with blank spaces.
