Hail claims

When hail damage totals your car: the payout, your loan, keeping it, and the title

Published 2026-11-26

After a bad storm, the adjuster looks at a car covered in dents, a cracked windshield, and maybe a caved-in roof, and comes back with a word you didn't expect: totaled. It can feel strange. The car still starts and drives. But a total loss is a money decision, not a statement that the car is undrivable.

The short version: your insurer can total a hail-damaged car when the repair bill gets close to what the car is worth. It then pays the car's actual cash value minus your comprehensive deductible, and if you have a loan, the lender is paid first. You can ask how the value was set, push back with your own evidence, and often keep the car if you tell the insurer early. In Texas, hail damage alone doesn't count toward a salvage title.

This page covers the total-loss process itself. If you're still deciding what to do with a dented car that wasn't totaled, see selling or trading in a car with unrepaired hail damage. For whether hail is covered at all, see is hail damage covered by comprehensive?

Why would hail total a car that still runs?

The Texas Department of Insurance's tip My car was totaled! Now what? explains the test: the insurer "will look at the value of your car vs. the cost to repair it." If the repair cost "is about the same or more than the value of your car, the insurance company will likely consider it totaled." TDI adds that "some companies might total your car even if the cost to fix it is lower."

Hail damage is spread across the hood, roof, trunk lid, fenders, and glass, so the repair bill can add up quickly. On an older car, a repair estimate that would be routine on a newer model can come close to what the car is worth. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) puts it simply on its auto insurance page: with an older car, "the cost of repairing the car can quickly exceed the worth of the car," and insurers will "'total' the car and pay you what the car was worth rather than fixing it."

The TDI pages we checked don't set a fixed percentage that triggers a total loss in Texas. You can ask the insurer what source it used to decide your car's value. TDI says so directly.

What the insurer pays

Hail is a comprehensive claim, so your comprehensive deductible comes off the top. (TDI's hail tip says that if you have comprehensive coverage on your auto policy, your insurer should pay for hail damage.)

TDI's auto insurance guide explains the rest: if the company totals your car, "it will pay your car's value minus depreciation." Its example: if your car is 10 years old, the company will pay "the value of a 10-year-old used car. It won't pay to replace your car with a new car that's the same make and model."

Your policy spells out the cap. In one real Texas personal auto policy we read (Redpoint County Mutual's form RCM.POL.2023.09), the damage-to-your-auto section says the company's limit "will be the lesser of the":

1. "Actual cash value of the stolen or damaged property;" 2. "Amount necessary to repair or replace the property with other of like kind and quality; or" 3. "Amount stated in the Declarations of this policy."

That same policy defines actual cash value as "the market value at the time of the loss less any depreciation," and says the payment "will be reduced by any applicable deductible." Your policy's wording may differ, so read your own Part D (or the physical damage section) and your declarations page.

What about the Blue Book? NAIC's FAQ answers that an insurer can pay less than Blue Book: "The Blue Book is only a guide. The company is required to pay you what your vehicle was actually worth (as a used car) the moment before the crash." The adjuster looks at what similar used cars are selling for in your area, and NAIC says "it's a good idea for you to independently research the value of your car too."

If you think the car is worth more

TDI says you "can try to negotiate," and to "be prepared to show what the car would sell for in your area." It suggests:

  • Get quotes from used car dealers.
  • Look at online prices and local ads for similar vehicles.
  • Document any special features or custom parts on your car.

For a Houston-area reader, that means listings for the same year, make, model, trim, and similar mileage near where you live, not a nationwide average. Ask the insurer for its valuation report and check it line by line: trim level, options, mileage, and condition. Send your evidence in writing and keep copies.

If talking it through doesn't work, TDI's auto complaint page and its not-paying-enough tip list your next options:

  • Appraisal. If your policy has an appraisal clause, you and the insurer each hire an appraiser, and an umpire settles differences. TDI says you pay for your appraiser and half the umpire's costs. The sample policy above says the appraisers "will state separately the actual cash value and the amount of loss." Our appraisal clause guide explains how it works and Texas's new appraisal law.
  • A complaint to TDI, in writing, through its online portal. TDI notes its ability to help "might be limited" and that it can't "determine damage amounts."
  • Court. TDI's auto guide notes that claims under $20,000 can go to Justice Court.

How fast the insurer has to move

TDI's auto Consumer Bill of Rights lists the deadlines when you file on your own policy:

  • Within 15 days after you file: acknowledge the claim, start investigating, and ask for anything else it needs.
  • Within 15 business days after it has everything it needs: approve or deny in writing (it can extend up to 45 days if it tells you it needs more time and why).
  • Within 5 business days after approving the claim: pay it.

The Bill of Rights adds that "TDI can extend these deadlines by 15 more days if there is a weather-related catastrophe." That matters after a big Houston-area hail storm, when insurers can be flooded with claims.

If you still owe money on the car

TDI's auto guide warns that if you owe on your car, the total-loss payment "might not be enough to pay off your loan. This will happen if the market value of your car is less than what you owe." Auto dealers and lenders usually offer gap insurance for that situation.

If your lender is listed on the policy, expect the money to go to it first. The sample policy's loss payee endorsement says damage payments "shall be paid as interest may appear to you and the loss payee shown in the Declarations." Call your lender early so you know the payoff amount and how any money left over gets to you.

If you have gap coverage, TDI's gap insurance tip says it "covers the difference between what you owe on your car and what it's worth." Read its exclusions. TDI lists some that can reduce what gap pays, including overdue payments, unpaid finance charges, warranty costs or balloon payments, "a deductible you have to pay before the insurance pays," and "damage from a previous accident." TDI also notes that gap products from a dealer or bank "might not be insurance," and TDI can't help with problems with those. If you disagree with a gap payment, TDI says to talk to the company before agreeing to the settlement and, if needed, ask for appraisal.

Rental cars after a hail total

Don't assume you'll have a rental car. TDI's auto guide says your own company pays for a rental only in certain situations, such as when you have rental reimbursement coverage, and "only for the time it believes is reasonable to repair or replace your car," up to your policy's limits. It also warns that if the company totals your car, "it might stop paying for the rental a few days after telling you."

Check whether your rental coverage applies to hail at all. The rental endorsement in the sample policy above applies only when "the loss is caused by an auto accident," and once a car is "deemed a total loss" it extends rental only 48 hours after an offer is made. NAIC's FAQ says that on a claim under your own policy, "your rental car rights are spelled out in your policy." Read yours, and line up other transportation as soon as you hear the word "totaled."

Can I keep the car?

Often, yes, but say so early. TDI: "If you want to keep your car, let the insurance company know as soon as possible. The insurance company will subtract the car's salvage value from the amount it was planning to pay you." NAIC says the insurer "has the option to take title to your vehicle when it issues payment" and "is entitled to any salvage value," but you "can, of course, negotiate with your company to purchase your vehicle for the agreed salvaged value."

Before you decide, ask the insurer for the salvage value it will deduct and run the numbers: the smaller check, plus whatever you'd spend to repair the car (or the fact that you'd keep driving it dented), versus taking the full payment and buying another car. If you have a loan, the lender has to agree, since the car is its collateral.

Keeping the car has another catch: dents that have already been paid for are part of the car's condition if hail hits again. Our selling or trading guide explains why a later claim generally won't pay for the same dents again.

What happens to the title

TDI's general totaled-car tip says a kept car "also may be issued a salvage title." That's true for wrecks, fires, floods, and other damage that counts. Hail is different in Texas:

  • Texas Transportation Code Sec. 501.091 defines a "salvage motor vehicle" by whether the cost to repair "damage" exceeds the car's actual cash value, and says "damage" does not include "sudden damage caused by hail" or "any damage caused only to the exterior paint."
  • TxDMV's Salvage/Nonrepairable Motor Vehicle Manual (January 2026) says repair costs related to hail damage "will not be included in the calculations," and that a salvage motor vehicle does not include "a motor vehicle for which an insurance company has paid a claim for the cost of repairing hail damage."
  • TxDMV's 2017 bulletin to insurers says "a voluntary application solely for hail damage is not acceptable even if damage has occurred to a major component part," and that such applications "will be rejected."

The duties Texas puts on insurers when they total a car are written for salvage and nonrepairable vehicles. Under Sec. 501.1001, an insurer that takes ownership of a salvage or nonrepairable vehicle by paying a claim must apply for that kind of title. Under Sec. 501.1002, an insurer that pays a claim on one and lets the owner keep it must report that to TxDMV within 30 days and tell the owner. A car damaged only by hail doesn't meet the salvage definition, so those branded-title steps shouldn't apply to it. If the car also has flood, collision, or other damage, the answer may be different. Ask your insurer what title action it plans to take, and call TxDMV at (888) 368-4689 if you're unsure.

A clean Texas title doesn't erase the history, though. TxDMV's Title Check page says a national title history report shows whether a vehicle was ever "declared a 'total loss' by an insurance company." If you keep the car and sell it later, tell the buyer about the hail total.

Will a hail total raise my rates?

A hail claim can show up in your claims history, but the auto Bill of Rights says your insurer can't refuse to renew your policy solely because of "claims involving damage from a weather-related incident that do not involve a collision, like damage from hail, wind, or flood." Our guide on whether a hail claim can raise your rates covers what Texas law allows.

A quick checklist when the adjuster says "totaled"

1. Ask for the valuation report and the source the insurer used for your car's value. 2. Check it for errors in trim, options, mileage, and condition. Gather local listings and dealer quotes for similar cars. 3. Decide quickly whether you want to keep the car, and ask for the salvage deduction in writing. 4. Call your lender for the payoff amount, and file a gap claim if you have gap coverage. 5. Read your rental coverage and plan for transportation. 6. Ask what title action the insurer will take, and remember that hail-only damage isn't salvage "damage" under Texas law. 7. If you can't agree on the value, look at appraisal, a written complaint to TDI, or Justice Court.
