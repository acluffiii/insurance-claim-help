Hail claims

Is hail damage covered by comprehensive, and how does the deductible work?

Published 2026-11-18

This is the mix-up that shows up most often after a storm: someone hears that hail is a "comprehensive" claim, looks at their deductible, and then finds out the number that actually applies is different — or that the word "comprehensive" does not appear on their home policy at all.

The short version is: on a car, hail is usually handled under comprehensive coverage, if you have that coverage, and you pay the comprehensive deductible. On a house, hail is a homeowners (or wind-and-hail) question, and the deductible may be separate from the one that applies to other kinds of damage. Your declarations page is the place to check.

If the hail hit your car

The Texas Department of Insurance auto insurance guide describes comprehensive coverage as paying "if your car is stolen or damaged by fire, flood, vandalism or something other than a collision." In the same guide's coverage table, "damage to your car because of fire, hail, theft, flood, flying gravel, or hitting an animal" is listed as something most policies cover if you have comprehensive coverage.

TDI's auto insurance glossary says the same thing in slightly different words: comprehensive coverage (physical damage other than collision) "pays for damage to or loss of your automobile from causes other than accidents. These include hail, vandalism, flood, fire, and theft."

Two practical points follow from that:

  • Comprehensive is optional coverage you buy. Liability coverage alone does not pay to repair your car after hail. If you still owe money on the car, TDI notes that your lender will generally require collision and comprehensive.
  • Collision and comprehensive often have separate deductibles. A hail claim uses the comprehensive deductible, not the collision deductible. Those amounts can be different.

TDI's auto guide explains a deductible as "the amount of a claim that you must pay yourself." Its example is a collision claim, but the arithmetic is the same: if a claim is $1,500 and the deductible for that coverage is $500, the company deducts $500 and pays $1,000.

TDI also says you must meet a deductible for comprehensive claims, and that the deductible is applied to each claim. A crash in one month and a hail claim later do not share one deductible.

If the hail hit your house

A home policy is not called "comprehensive" in the same way an auto policy is. TDI's after-storm tip puts the two side by side: "If you have wind and hail coverage on your home policy or comprehensive coverage on your auto policy, your insurance company should pay for your hail damage."

Standard homeowners insurance often does include hail, but you still have to read the policy. The Insurance Information Institute's Am I Covered? page says a typical HO-3 policy provides coverage for hail, and adds that "in areas prone to hailstorms, you may have a specific hail damage deductible."

That separate deductible is the other half of the confusion. TDI's page on how to read a declarations page says a home policy "will show separate deductibles for your house and its contents" and that "you might have separate deductibles for damage from wind and hail (tornadoes and hurricanes) and 'other perils' like fire and theft."

The Insurance Information Institute's deductibles article says wind/hail deductibles "are most common in places that typically experience severe windstorms and hail," including Texas, and "are most commonly paid in percentages, typically from 1 percent to 5 percent."

A percentage is not a small difference. TDI's deductibles tip walks through two homes insured for $150,000 with $6,500 in roof repairs after a hail storm:

  • Policy A has a $500 deductible. The company pays $6,000.
  • Policy B has a 5 percent deductible, which TDI calculates as $7,500. The company pays nothing, because the repairs are less than the deductible.

Those figures are TDI's examples, not a prediction of your claim. They exist to show why you should convert a percentage deductible into dollars before a storm, using the limit listed on your declarations page.

A Texas-coast caveat

Even "most policies cover hail" is not universal. TDI's home insurance shopping tips say: "If you live on the Texas coast or in Harris County on Galveston Bay, your home policy might not cover wind and hail damage. The Texas Windstorm Insurance Association sells wind and hail coverage on the coast." That is a location-specific gap, not a rule for every Houston-area address. If you are near the bay or the coast, confirm whether wind and hail are on the home policy or in a separate policy.

How to check your own numbers

1. Pull the declarations page for the auto policy, the home policy, or both. 2. For a car, find comprehensive coverage and the deductible printed next to it. Confirm it is not the same line as collision. 3. For a house, look for a wind, hail, or wind/hail deductible, and notice whether it is a dollar amount or a percentage. 4. If it is a percentage, do the math against the dwelling limit shown on that page, the way TDI's example does. 5. Remember TDI's rule that a home or auto deductible is applied per claim, not once per year.

Should you file?

This site cannot tell you whether to file a claim. TDI does note, on the same deductibles page, that "filing small claims may affect how much you have to pay for insurance later." That is a reason to know the deductible first, not a rule to file or not file.

If you do have hail damage and you decide to claim it, TDI's after hail or windstorms tip is the official starting point: file as soon as you can, photograph the damage, prevent further damage, keep receipts, and be available when the adjuster visits. TDI also warns against contractors who offer to waive a homeowners deductible, which it says is illegal under Texas law.
