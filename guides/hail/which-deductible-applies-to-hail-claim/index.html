Hail claims

Which deductible applies to a hail claim? All-other-perils vs. wind/hail at home, comprehensive vs. collision on a car

Published 2026-11-27

A common surprise after a hail storm: you remember your deductible as $1,000, but the claim letter subtracts a much bigger number. Usually that's because a home policy can have more than one deductible, and the one used depends on what caused the damage. Cars have their own version of this, with separate comprehensive and collision deductibles.

The short version: on a home policy, hail damage usually falls under the wind/hail deductible if your policy has one, not the "all other perils" deductible. That wind/hail deductible is often a percentage, and in Texas a private insurer's declarations page has to show it as an exact dollar amount. On a car, hail is a comprehensive claim, so the comprehensive deductible applies, not the collision one. If you think the wrong one was used, ask the insurer in writing to explain, and you can file a complaint with TDI.

Home policies: "all other perils" vs. wind/hail

The Texas Department of Insurance (TDI) page on reading a declarations page puts it plainly: "You might have separate deductibles for damage from wind and hail (tornadoes and hurricanes) and 'other perils' like fire and theft."

So a home policy may list two deductibles:

  • Wind/hail deductible. Used when the loss is caused by windstorm or hail.
  • All other perils (AOP) deductible. Used for other covered losses, such as fire or theft.

Here is how one real Texas policy says it. The Texas FAIR Plan Association homeowners policy (2018 edition) has two deductible clauses. Clause 1, "Windstorm or Hail," applies its declarations-page amount to each loss under Coverage A (Dwelling) and Coverage B (Personal Property) "that results from windstorm or hail." Clause 2, "All Other Perils," applies to each loss under those coverages "unless the loss results from windstorm or hail." It adds that if a single event causes loss by windstorm or hail and loss by lightning, "only the larger deductible will apply."

Your policy may word this differently, or it may have only one deductible for everything. The point is that the cause of the damage decides which deductible applies, so the deductible on a hail claim may not be the one you remember.

The Insurance Information Institute (III) says wind/hail deductibles "are most common in places that typically experience severe windstorms and hail," and names Texas among them. It adds that they "are most commonly paid in percentages, typically from 1 percent to 5 percent" (Understanding your insurance deductibles).

Where to find your deductibles

Start with the declarations page, the summary at the front of your policy. TDI's home insurance guide says it "has a summary of your policy, including your coverages, dollar limits, and deductibles."

Texas law helps here. For residential property policies from private insurers, Texas Insurance Code Sec. 2301.056 says the declarations page must:

1. list and identify each type of deductible, and 2. state "the exact dollar amount of each deductible."

If a policy provision or endorsement can change a deductible's dollar amount, the declarations page (or a separate disclosure page right after it) has to point to that provision, and the provision has to explain how the change is figured. TDI's rule, 28 TAC §5.9700, adds that the insurer must label each deductible with a short description such as "Wind and Hail," and must issue a new declarations page at renewal if a deductible's dollar amount changes. TDI's order adopting that rule says "no deductibles are exempt." The homeowners Bill of Rights insurers must give you says the same thing in plain words: "Your company must list the exact dollar amount of each deductible in your policy."

Two notes on that law:

  • The definition of "insurer" in that part of the code (Sec. 2301.051) leaves out the Texas Windstorm Insurance Association (TWIA) and the Texas FAIR Plan Association. Their declarations pages still show your deductible. TWIA's Inside a Policy page points to it on the sample declarations page.
  • When you look, check the labels. Look for a line that says something like "Wind and Hail" or "Windstorm or Hail" and a separate "All Other Perils" line. If you only see one deductible, ask your agent or company whether it applies to every cause of loss.

The NAIC's post-disaster claims guide suggests asking your insurer, when you report the claim: "Are there separate deductibles for hail, hurricane, or wind damage? What are those?"

How a percentage deductible is figured

A percentage deductible is a percentage of the insured value, not of the damage. III explains: "if your house is insured for $100,000 and your insurance policy has a 2 percent deductible, $2,000 would be deducted from any claim payment."

TDI's deductibles tip compares two homes insured for $150,000, each with $6,500 in roof repairs after hail. Policy A has a $500 deductible and pays $6,000. Policy B has a 5 percent deductible, or $7,500, and "would not pay anything because the amount of repairs is less than the deductible."

Illustrative example only (made-up numbers, not a statistic): say a house is insured for $300,000, with a 2% wind/hail deductible and a $1,000 all-other-perils deductible.

  • 2% of $300,000 = $6,000 wind/hail deductible.
  • If covered hail damage came to $14,000, the deductible would bring the payment down to $8,000 ($14,000 − $6,000), before any other policy adjustments such as depreciation.
  • The $1,000 all-other-perils deductible wouldn't be used for that hail loss, because the policy assigns hail to the wind/hail deductible.

Your own numbers come from your declarations page. Under the Texas law above, a private insurer's page should already show the dollar figure, so you don't have to work out the percentage yourself.

Hurricanes, named storms, and the Texas coast

Some states use separate "hurricane" or "named storm" deductibles. III's background on hurricane and windstorm deductibles says that in Texas, "the windstorm deductible applies to windstorm and hail damage from any type of wind storm, not only named storms or hurricanes."

On the coast, the home policy may not cover wind and hail at all. TDI's home insurance guide says that if you live on the Texas coast or in Harris County on Galveston Bay, "your home policy might not cover wind and hail damage," and TWIA sells that coverage through local agents. TWIA says its policies cover "only windstorm and hail damages." TDI's TWIA overview (October 2026) lists residential deductible options of $100, $250, or 1%, with optional larger deductibles of 1.5%, 2%, 2.5%, 3%, 4%, or 5%. TWIA's declarations page shows a "Per Item/Per Occ Deductible," which TWIA describes as "the deductible that applies to each item listed on the declaration page per loss." If your home policy leaves out wind and hail and you buy that coverage from TWIA, a hail claim on the house goes to TWIA and uses the TWIA deductible.

The Texas FAIR Plan has also changed its options. TFPA announced that on or after July 1, 2026, the 1% wind and hail deductible option is going away for its homeowners policies (existing 1% policies "will automatically change to 2% at policy renewal"). The remaining options are 2% to 5% for wind and hail and 1% to 5% for all other perils, and split deductibles are allowed.

Cars: comprehensive, not collision

On an auto policy, hail is a comprehensive loss. TDI's auto insurance glossary says comprehensive coverage "pays for damage to or loss of your automobile from causes other than accidents. These include hail, vandalism, flood, fire, and theft." TDI's auto insurance guide lists hail damage as covered "if you have comprehensive coverage."

Policy language says the same. A sample Texas Personal Auto Policy defines collision, then lists losses that "are not considered 'collision,'" including "Hail, water or flood" and "Windstorm."

What this means for your deductible:

  • Comprehensive and collision each have their own deductible. TDI's auto guide says you pay a deductible for collision, comprehensive, and uninsured/underinsured motorist claims. A hail claim uses the comprehensive deductible shown on your declarations page.
  • The deductible applies to each claim. TDI says, "For home and auto policies, the deductible will be applied to each claim."
  • No comprehensive coverage means no hail coverage for your car. Liability-only coverage doesn't pay to fix your own car. TDI's after-storm tip says your insurer should pay for hail damage "if you have... comprehensive coverage on your auto policy."
  • Watch for endorsements. Endorsements can change deductibles. For example, the sample auto policy above includes an optional endorsement that doubles the comprehensive and collision deductibles during the first 90 days of a new policy, or a rewrite after a lapse. Read any endorsements listed with your declarations page.

If you think the wrong deductible was applied

1. Read the claim letter and your declarations page side by side. Find which deductible the insurer used, and check that it matches the dollar amount on your declarations page. 2. Ask in writing. Ask the insurer which deductible it applied, which policy provision it relied on, and how it decided the cause of the damage (for example, hail versus something else). Under the homeowners Bill of Rights, your company "must tell you in writing why your claim or part of your claim was denied." TDI's disagreement tip says to tell the company why you disagree and send supporting documents. Keep copies of everything. 3. Know what appraisal does. The Bill of Rights describes appraisal as a way to settle "a dispute about the amount to repair or replace your damaged property." That's a dispute about the dollar amount of the loss. A question about which deductible your policy calls for is a question about how the policy reads, so a written explanation and a complaint are more direct routes. 4. File a complaint with TDI. TDI can help with complaints about insurance companies, agents, and adjusters. Use the home complaint page or the auto complaint page, and upload your letters and declarations page. TDI says companies have 15 days to respond to it and can ask for a 10-day extension. 5. Other options. The Bill of Rights also lists talking to an attorney or hiring a licensed public adjuster. See our guides on public adjusters and the appraisal clause.

Before the next storm

  • Find your declarations page and write down every deductible and what it applies to.
  • If your wind/hail deductible is a percentage, check the dollar amount shown. TDI's example shows how a 5% deductible can be bigger than a whole roof repair.
  • On your car, check that you have comprehensive coverage and what its deductible is.
  • When you shop or renew, ask about deductible choices. TDI notes that "the higher the deductible, the lower the cost for the policy," but you pay more out of pocket when you file a claim.
