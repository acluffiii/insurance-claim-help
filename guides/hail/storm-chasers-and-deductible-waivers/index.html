Storm-chaser contractors and deductible waivers after a hail storm

Published 2026-11-22

After a big hail storm, contractors often show up fast: door knockers, flyers, yard signs, free roof inspections. Some are local and legitimate. Some aren't. One pitch comes up again and again: "We'll take care of your deductible."

The short version: in Texas, it's illegal for a contractor to offer to waive, absorb, or rebate your home insurance deductible, or to work it into the bid. You are required to pay your deductible, and your insurance company can ask for proof that you did. If a contractor makes that offer, TDI's advice is simple: hire someone else.

What the law says

Texas tightened this in 2019 (House Bill 2102, effective September 1, 2019). There are two main pieces:

  • You have to pay it. Texas Insurance Code Sec. 707.002: "A person insured under a property insurance policy shall pay any deductible applicable to a first-party claim made under the policy."
  • The seller can't help you skip it. Business and Commerce Code Sec. 27.02 makes it an offense for someone selling goods or services paid from a property insurance claim to advertise, promise, or actually, without the insurer's consent, "pay, waive, absorb, or otherwise decline to charge or collect" the deductible, give "a rebate or credit" that offsets it, or "in any other manner assist the insured in avoiding monetary payment" of it. It's a Class B misdemeanor. TDI's 2019 announcement says violators "could get up to a $2,000 fine and up to six months in jail."

These laws are written for property insurance policies that cover real property, such as a home. The statute's definition (Insurance Code Sec. 707.001) doesn't mention car policies. TDI's hail tip puts it this way: "It's illegal to waive a homeowners deductible under state law."

The notice you should see in your contract

Sec. 27.02 also says a contract for $1,000 or more that's expected to be paid wholly or partly from a property insurance claim must include this notice in at least 12-point bold type:

"Texas law requires a person insured under a property insurance policy to pay any deductible applicable to a claim made under the policy. It is a violation of Texas law for a seller of goods or services who reasonably expects to be paid wholly or partly from the proceeds of a property insurance claim to knowingly allow the insured person to fail to pay, or assist the insured person's failure to pay, the applicable insurance deductible."

If a roofing contract for an insurance job is missing that notice, that's worth asking about.

How "covering your deductible" usually works, and why it hurts you

TDI's deductible tip describes the common methods:

  • An estimate "that's higher than the actual cost to repair your home or roof," with the extra insurance money used to cover the deductible.
  • "Rebates or credits in the amount of your deductible." TDI: "That's also illegal."

Other names you may hear include a "deductible discount," "we'll work it into the bid," or a "referral credit" equal to your deductible. TDI's scam signs page lists "work it into the bid" by name.

Why it's your problem too, according to TDI:

  • A contractor doing this "might be sending false information to your insurance company about the cost of repairs. That would be fraud."
  • The contractor "is likely making up the difference by cutting corners or using lower quality products. That can lead to more claims and repairs later."

Your insurer can ask for proof you paid

TDI says your insurance company "can ask for proof that you paid your deductible before it pays the full amount of your claim," such as "a canceled check, money order receipt, credit card statement, or a copy of the payment plan with your contractor."

Insurance Code Sec. 707.004 ties this to replacement cost policies: the insurer may hold back withheld recoverable depreciation or a replacement cost holdback until it gets reasonable proof the deductible was paid. If your roof claim is being paid in two checks, our guide to replacement cost vs. actual cash value explains that second check. A financing or installment plan that requires full payment of the deductible over time counts as proof under that section.

Other warning signs after a hail storm

TDI's contracting scams tip and scam signs page flag these:

  • Signing something "just to inspect." "Scammers will try to get you to sign a contract by calling it an estimate or a release just to go on your roof." Read it, and make sure it isn't a contract.
  • Blank spaces. Don't sign a contract with blanks. They "could fill in the blank sections later with higher costs or different work."
  • "The value of insurance proceeds." TDI warns against contracts saying "Homeowner agrees to the listed repairs for the value of insurance proceeds," because "the contractor gets every penny from your insurance payment." The contract should list the materials.
  • Big money up front. Be wary of a large or full down payment. After a declared disaster, Business and Commerce Code Chapter 58 says a covered disaster remediation contractor "may not require a person to make a full or partial payment under a contract before the contractor begins work." That chapter doesn't apply to a contractor that has kept a physical business address for at least a year in your county or a neighboring county, and it applies only after a governor's or county judge's disaster declaration.
  • A roofer who says they'll handle your claim for you. TDI's roofing and insurance page says Texas doesn't allow a roofer or contractor "to act as a public insurance adjuster on insurance claims if they're also doing the work." Examples TDI lists as improper include "Offering to negotiate claim settlements or file a claim for the policyholder" and promising to recover "every dime you are owed."

How to pick a contractor, per TDI

  • Get at least three written bids on company letterhead with a phone number and address. TDI notes your adjuster "can give you an idea of what the repair should cost."
  • Use local companies when possible, and verify the local address and phone number.
  • Check references, the Better Business Bureau, and online complaints.
  • Ask for a certificate of insurance and bond, and call the companies listed to verify.
  • Be wary of door-to-door solicitors and of a bid that's far lower than the rest.
  • Ask the contractor to tell you if they contact your insurance company about additional work or costs.
  • Pay by check or credit card, not cash, and don't make the final payment until the work is done.

What about a car?

The deductible-waiver statute above is written for policies covering real property, so it doesn't clearly reach auto comprehensive claims. The same practical caution applies, though: TDI's hail tip tells drivers to get more than one estimate and check whether a contractor or body shop has complaints. If any shop's offer depends on sending your insurer a number that's higher than the real repair cost, that's the same false-information problem TDI describes above. Ask your insurance company before agreeing to any deal involving your deductible.

If someone offers to waive your deductible

1. Don't sign. TDI: "find a new contractor." 2. Talk to your insurance company. 3. Report it to the Texas Attorney General's Consumer Protection Hotline at 800-621-0508. TDI also says to report possible contracting scams to local law enforcement.

Questions about your rights or your claim? TDI's Help Line is 800-252-3439 (8 a.m. to 5 p.m. Central, Monday to Friday).
