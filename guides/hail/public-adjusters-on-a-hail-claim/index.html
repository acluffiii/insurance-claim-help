Public adjusters on a hail claim: what they are, what they cost, and questions to ask

Published 2026-11-25

After a big hail storm, homeowners often hear the same pitch: "Don't deal with the insurance company alone. Hire a public adjuster." Some people find one genuinely helpful. Others sign a contract in a hurry and later wonder what they agreed to.

The short version: a public adjuster is a licensed professional you hire, and pay, to work your claim for you. In Texas their fee can't be more than 10% of the insurance settlement, and it can be figured on the whole settlement, not just the extra money they get. They can't also be your roofer, can't give legal advice, and can't stop you from talking to your insurer. You can cancel the contract in writing within 72 hours of signing, but not after that.

What is a public adjuster?

There are three kinds of people who might "adjust" your hail claim:

  • The insurance company's adjuster (staff or independent) works for the insurer.
  • A public insurance adjuster works for you. Texas requires their contract to say, in bold type, "WE REPRESENT THE INSURED ONLY" (Insurance Code Sec. 4102.103).
  • An appraiser is hired by each side only if the policy's appraisal process is used to settle a dispute about the amount. See The appraisal clause.

From TDI's public adjuster tip page: "You can hire a public insurance adjuster to negotiate with your insurance company to settle your claim. Public insurance adjusters understand the claims process and are licensed by TDI."

TDI's page is written for homeowners, and describes hiring one as an option "if your homeowners claim is denied or you think your insurance company should pay more for repairs." Texas law defines a public adjuster as someone who works on claims "under any policy of insurance covering real or personal property" (Sec. 4102.001), but in practice the TDI guidance is aimed at home claims. If you're thinking about one for a car, ask directly whether they handle auto claims.

You don't have to hire one. TDI's required contract form (FIN535) says: "YOU DO NOT HAVE TO ENTER THIS CONTRACT TO MAKE A CLAIM FOR LOSS OR DAMAGE ON A POLICY OF INSURANCE."

What do they cost?

Texas caps the fee. Insurance Code Sec. 4102.104 lets a public adjuster charge "an hourly fee, a flat rate, a percentage of the total amount paid by an insurer to resolve a claim, or another method of compensation," but "the total commission received may not exceed 10 percent of the amount of the insurance settlement on the claim." TDI's contract form says that 10% includes "expenses, direct costs, or any other costs accrued by the public insurance adjuster."

Three things surprise people:

1. The fee can be based on the whole settlement, not just the increase. TDI's own example: "If the company wants to pay $100,000 for your claim and you're disputing $20,000, the public adjuster could charge $10,000." 2. You might owe a fee even if the offer doesn't go up. TDI: "If your insurance company doesn't increase its offer after you hire the public adjuster, you might still have to pay the public adjuster. And you still have to pay your deductible." 3. You can ask for a dollar amount and negotiate. TDI: "You can ask the public adjuster to put their fee in a dollar amount, instead of a percentage, in the contract," and "You can try to negotiate a lower fee."

One more rule: if the insurer pays, or commits in writing to pay, the policy limit within 72 hours after the loss is reported, the public adjuster can't take a percentage. They're limited to reasonable pay for their time and expenses up to that point (Sec. 4102.104(b), repeated in the FIN535 contract).

Before you sign, it's worth asking what the claim might pay without help and what the fee would be in dollars under the contract. For a hail claim where the insurer already agreed to a fair number, a percentage fee may cost more than it gains. For a large or complicated loss, or a denial you think is wrong, the math can look different. Only you can weigh that.

What a public adjuster can't do in Texas

From TDI's tip page and Chapter 4102:

  • Be your contractor. A public adjuster can't "participate directly or indirectly in the reconstruction, repair, or restoration of damaged property" on a claim they adjust, or take money from or have a financial interest in a repair, construction, or salvage firm getting work on that claim (Sec. 4102.158). The flip side: a contractor can't act as a public adjuster or advertise to adjust claims on property they're repairing or may repair, even with a power of attorney (Sec. 4102.163). If your roofer offers to "handle your insurance claim" as part of the job, that's a red flag. See also Storm-chaser contractors and deductible waivers.
  • Take referral fees. TDI says they can't "get referral fees from attorneys, contractors, or anyone else involved in your claim" (see also Sec. 4102.164).
  • Solicit at all hours. They can only solicit business between 9 a.m. and 9 p.m. Monday through Saturday and noon to 9 p.m. on Sunday, unless you contacted them first (Sec. 4102.152). TDI also says they can't knock on your door asking for business during a natural disaster.
  • Wear a badge while working as a public adjuster (Sec. 4102.154).
  • Practice law or give legal advice, or work on health care claims.
  • Keep you from talking with your insurance company.

Check the license before anything else

Anyone acting as a public adjuster in Texas needs a TDI license, with narrow exceptions for Texas attorneys and for a licensed agent helping with a policy that agent sold (Sec. 4102.051). The contract itself must show the adjuster's license number on every page (28 TAC §19.708).

Ways to check:

  • Call TDI's Help Line at 800-252-3439. TDI says the Help Line can tell you whether the adjuster is licensed, how many complaints have been filed against them, and whether TDI has taken disciplinary action.
  • Look up the license by name or number. TDI's license lookup page points to the Customer Inquiry form on Sircon.com.

TDI also suggests asking friends and family who've used one and checking online consumer reviews.

Reading the contract

Texas requires public adjusters to use a contract form approved by TDI (Sec. 4102.103). TDI publishes the prescribed FIN535 contract. Under 28 TAC §19.708 and that form, the contract should show:

  • The adjuster's name, address, phone, and license number, and the date and time it was signed.
  • The fee, and how it's calculated (hourly, flat, percentage, or another method), with a detailed explanation.
  • That the adjuster will provide an invoice listing services and costs.
  • These notices in bold: you may cancel by written notice within 72 hours of signing for any reason; "We represent the insured only"; and that you don't have to sign to make a claim.
  • How payments the insurer made before you signed will be treated when figuring the fee, if that applies.

The 72-hour window is short. TDI: "If you change your mind, you have 72 hours after you've signed a contract to cancel it. You can't get out of the contract after 72 hours." The FIN535 form says notice must be in writing, by registered or certified mail with return receipt requested to the address on the contract, or delivered in person. Keep proof.

Questions to ask before you sign

These come from TDI's checklist, plus the rules above:

1. Are you licensed by TDI? What's your license number? (Then check it.) 2. Will you inspect my damaged property yourself? 3. Will you talk to my insurance company and ask for a larger amount? 4. How long do you expect it to take to settle my claim? 5. What's your fee, and what is it in dollars on what the insurer has already offered? Will you write it as a dollar amount? 6. Does the fee apply to money the insurer already paid or offered before I signed? 7. Do you have any connection to a roofer, contractor, body shop, attorney, appraiser, or salvage company on this claim? 8. Do you handle this kind of claim (home roof, or auto, if that's what you have)?

Other options if you think the insurer is paying too little

A public adjuster is one option, not the only one. TDI's "What if my insurance isn't paying enough?" page lists others: sending the insurer your contractor's estimate and asking it to take another look, appraisal, mediation, a complaint to TDI, or court. Several can be done without paying a percentage of your claim. For a denied roof claim, see Why roof hail claims get denied.

If you have a problem with a public adjuster you hired, TDI says you can report them by calling 800-252-3439 or filing a complaint, and that you have the right to sue. For legal questions, talk to a Texas attorney.

Questions about your policy or a claim? Call TDI's Help Line at 800-252-3439.
