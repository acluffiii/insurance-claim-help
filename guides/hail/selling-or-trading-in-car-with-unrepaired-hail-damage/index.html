Hail claims

Selling or trading in a car with unrepaired hail damage

Published 2026-11-23

A hail storm leaves your car covered in dents, and you'd rather sell it or trade it in than fix it. The questions pile up: Can I keep the insurance money? Does my lender get it? Will the dealer make me file a claim? What happens if it hails on the same car again? Do I have to tell a buyer?

The short version: whether you can take a hail payment and leave the car unrepaired depends on your policy and, if you have a loan or lease, on your lender. Once damage has been paid for, it's generally treated as damage the car already had, so don't expect a later claim to pay for the same dents again. Be upfront with any buyer. And in Texas, hail damage alone doesn't count toward a salvage title under state law, even if the insurer totals the car.

Can I keep the hail payment and not fix the car?

TDI's auto insurance guide explains how a repair claim is paid: an adjuster estimates the cost of repairs, the company "will base its payment on the adjuster's estimate," and it must send you a check within five business days after it agrees to pay. Insurers pay for repairs "only up to its actual cash value," which is the cost to replace your car minus depreciation.

The TDI pages we checked don't say you're required to spend a hail payment on repairs. They also don't say you're free not to. That depends on:

  • Your policy. Read the physical damage (comprehensive) section, or ask your insurer how it handles a claim where you don't repair.
  • Your loan or lease. See the next section.

If you own the car outright, ask your insurer directly before you decide, and get the answer in writing if you can.

If you still owe money on the car

TDI says that if you still owe money on your car, "your lender will require you to have collision and comprehensive coverage." That's because the car is the lender's collateral.

The federal bank regulator's consumer site, HelpWithMyBank.gov, says an insurance check "may be payable to both the policy holder (you) and the lien holder (the bank)." Its advice: "notify your bank to determine what steps should be taken, including whether you should send the check to the bank."

In practice, that means your lender may have a say in whether the car gets repaired or the money goes toward the loan. Your loan or lease contract controls this. Call the lender before you sell, trade in, or decide to skip repairs.

If the car is totaled and you owe more than it's worth, TDI notes the insurance payment "might not be enough to pay off your loan," and that gap insurance is designed for that situation.

Will a later hail claim pay for the same dents?

Generally, don't count on it. TDI says insurers pay for repairs only up to the car's actual cash value. If you were already paid for dents and didn't fix them, those dents were part of the car's condition when the next storm hit, and an insurer may take that into account.

The Texas sources we checked don't spell out exactly how an insurer must handle unrepaired, already-paid damage on a later claim. That depends on your policy and your insurer. For a sense of how one state regulator explains the idea, New Jersey's Department of Banking and Insurance says an insurer can deduct for damage that existed before a loss, and that "if your car was damaged in a previous accident and you decided not to get it repaired ... your car would not be worth as much on the open market." New Jersey's rules aren't Texas rules, so ask your own insurer how it would treat old damage.

Two plain cautions:

  • Don't claim the same damage twice. TDI's auto guide lists filing "a fraudulent claim" as a reason an insurer may cancel a policy at any time.
  • Keep your paperwork. Save the estimate, photos, and payment record from the hail claim. If a new storm hits, they help show which damage is new.

Selling the car yourself

Tell the buyer about the hail damage and about any claim. Texas's Deceptive Trade Practices Act (Business and Commerce Code Sec. 17.46) lists, among false, misleading, or deceptive acts "in the conduct of any trade or commerce," "failing to disclose information concerning goods or services which was known at the time of the transaction" when the failure was meant to get the buyer into a deal they wouldn't otherwise have made. Whether that law reaches your particular sale is a legal question. The simple, safe course is to disclose the damage, put it in writing, and price the car with it in mind.

Buyers can check a car's history. TxDMV's Title Check page says a report from the national title database (NMVTIS) shows the vehicle's title history, "which includes whether the vehicle was ever in the possession of a junk or salvage yard or declared a 'total loss' by an insurance company."

Finish the paperwork. TxDMV's buying or selling page says vehicles must be titled in the buyer's name within 30 days of the sale, and recommends the seller go with the buyer to the county tax office. It also recommends filing a Vehicle Transfer Notification. If you file it within 30 days of the sale, "you cannot be held responsible for parking tickets and toll violations" committed by the buyer. If there's a loan, talk to your lender about paying it off and getting a release of lien, which TxDMV lists among the documents a buyer may need.

Trading in at a dealer

Expect the dealer to look at the dents. As the New Jersey regulator puts it, unrepaired damage means the car "would not be worth as much on the open market." How much less is between you and the dealer. We aren't citing any trade-in figures, because none of the official sources we checked give one.

Some sellers say a dealer asked them to file a hail claim before trading in. Keep a few things in mind:

  • It's your policy and your decision. A claim is for a covered loss, such as hail under comprehensive coverage. TDI's auto guide notes that most companies use the CLUE database to learn your claims history, and that a company "can charge you more or refuse to sell you a policy" based on that report. Our guide on whether a hail claim can raise your rates explains what Texas law allows.
  • If the damage was already paid on an earlier claim, don't file again for it. See the caution above.
  • Do the deductible math first. Our guide on filing when damage is close to your deductible walks through it.
  • If you have a loan, the lender is part of this. Ask the lender how a trade-in payoff works, and remember any hail check may be payable to you and the lender.

TxDMV also notes that when you trade in or sell to a licensed dealer, the dealer isn't required to title the car in the dealership's name, so "the vehicle stays in your name until it is sold to an individual." TxDMV recommends filing a Vehicle Transfer Notification to protect yourself.

If the insurer totals the car for hail

TDI's totaled-car tip says an insurer will likely total a car when the repair cost is "about the same or more than the value of your car," and some companies might total it even if the cost to fix it is lower. If you want to keep the car, tell the insurer as soon as possible. "The insurance company will subtract the car's salvage value from the amount it was planning to pay you."

What about the title? Here's the part many people don't expect:

  • Texas Transportation Code Sec. 501.091 defines a "salvage motor vehicle" by the cost to repair "damage," and that definition of "damage" specifically excludes "sudden damage caused by hail" and "any damage caused only to the exterior paint."
  • TxDMV's Salvage/Nonrepairable Motor Vehicle Manual (January 2026) says repair costs "related to hail damage" aren't counted, and that a salvage motor vehicle "does not include ... a motor vehicle for which an insurance company has paid a claim for the cost of repairing hail damage."
  • A 2017 TxDMV bulletin told insurers that "a voluntary application solely for hail damage is not acceptable even if damage has occurred to a major component part," and that such applications "will be rejected."

So under Texas law, hail damage alone shouldn't put a salvage brand on a Texas title, even when the insurer pays the car out as a total loss. TDI's general totaled-car tip does say a kept car "may" get a salvage title, which is true for wrecks, fires, floods, and other damage that does count. If your car has other damage besides hail, or it's titled in another state, the answer may be different. Ask your insurer which title action it plans to take, and call TxDMV at (888) 368-4689 if you're unsure.

Even without a salvage brand, a title history report can show whether a car was "declared a 'total loss' by an insurance company," per TxDMV's Title Check page. Plan to disclose a hail total to a buyer.

For a car that does qualify as salvage, Texas law is strict. Under Secs. 501.09111 and 501.1002, the owner of a salvage vehicle may not register or drive it on a public highway, and can't sell it until a salvage title has been issued. After repairs, the owner applies for a rebuilt title under Sec. 501.100, which "must describe or disclose the motor vehicle's former condition."

A quick checklist before you sell or trade

1. Find your claim paperwork: estimate, photos, and payment. 2. Call your lender if you have a loan or lease. Ask what it requires for a hail check and for a sale or trade-in. 3. Ask your insurer how it treats unrepaired damage, and, if the car was totaled, what title action it took. 4. Tell any buyer about the damage and any claim, in writing. 5. Finish the title transfer and file a Vehicle Transfer Notification with TxDMV.

Questions about your auto policy or a claim? TDI's Help Line is 800-252-3439. For title questions, TxDMV is at (888) 368-4689.
